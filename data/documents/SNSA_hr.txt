1 INTRODUCTION 
 
1.1 PROJECT BACKGROUND 
Slovenian Nuclear Safety Administration (SNSA) is a nuclear regulatory authority in Slovenia established in 1987. 
It operates under the auspices of the Ministry of Natural Resources and Spatial Planning. SNSA is responsible for 
performing expert, administrative, supervisory and development tasks in the areas of nuclear and radiation 
safety, the implementation of radiation practices and the activities of using sources of ionizing radiation (except 
in healthcare or veterinary medicine), environmental protection against ionizing radiation, physical protection 
of nuclear substances and facilities, non-proliferation of nuclear weapons and protection of nuclear goods.  
In addition to that, the SNSA is also very much involved internationally as an active member of various 
organizations (e.g., IAEA, OECD/NEA, ENSREG, WENRA, etc.), to which it reports, actively participates in peer 
reviews of other regulators (e.g., IRRS, ARTEMIS, EPREV, TPR and other peer review missions), or in working 
groups for various expert tasks. 
SNSA supervises several nuclear and radiation facilities, including the Krško NPP, the TRIGA Mark II research 
reactor, the central radioactive waste storage for low and intermediate level solid radio-active waste from 
institutional users, the Žirovski Vrh Uranium Mine (in decommissioning), the mining waste disposal site Jazbec, 
and Boršt uranium mill tailings disposal site, as well as hundreds of users of radiation sources. 
Furthermore, SNSA is also the main developer of legislation in the areas of nuclear and radiation safety, where 
it drafts and amends laws and regulations to be adopted by the Government or the Ministries. Likewise, SNSA 
has the right (and obligation) to develop guidance documents (so called “practical guidelines”), which guide the 
operators of nuclear and radiation facilities, and users of radiation sources on how to implement the regulatory 
requirements given in acts and regulations. 
SNSA performs all these tasks with an admirably low number of staff. SNSA regularly employs around 40 staff, 
of whom around 35 are experts in certain technical areas, and the rest are administrative and legal support. 
SNSA has had the same number of staff for the last 20 years, but the nature of the work performed by SNSA is 
such that the number of tasks is constantly growing; number of source users to be supervised is constantly 
increasing, the number and types of objects that need licensing and regular inspections is growing, and 
international cooperation is becoming more and more intense, requiring regular and increased reporting, 
collaboration on development of new state-of-the-art requirements, and cooperating in peer reviews.  
In 2007 SNSA developed an integrated management system (IMS) and has been maintaining it ever since. Today 
the SNSA’s IMS contains over 160 different procedures, which direct the workflow of SNSA processes in a 
manner to use the resources (human and financial) as efficiently as possible. Every year SNSA conducts internal 
audits and management reviews (one per year) of the management system in search of opportunities for 
additional improvements to its processes in order to make the most of every employee. However, even the 
constant improvements of processes have their limit, and increasing workload cannot be successfully managed 
with the same number of people, without degrading the quality of the work. 
This is precisely why SNSA decided to carry out this project with the purpose to obtain an independent opinion 
and perspective on its operation and ideas for possible future development.

1.2 PROJECT OBJECTIVES AND OUTPUTS 
The objective of this project is to carry out an independent and unbiased review of SNSA's organization and all 
the tasks that certain organizational units and employees perform and, based on a comparison with foreign 
modern regulatory bodies that perform a similar number of tasks and in the context of a similarly large nuclear 
program, to prepare a proposal of a long-term human resource plan that would enable SNSA to efficiently and 
effectively perform all of its mandated tasks with the highest level of quality. 
 
The main outputs of the project shall include: 
 review of current SNSA organization, number of staff, responsibilities and tasks performed by SNSA and 
its organizational units; 
 comparison with similar organizations, overview of their responsibilities and scope of tasks performed; 
 analysis of staff required to perform all prescribed SNSA tasks, their required education and 
competencies; 
 preparation of a proposal of long-term human resource plan together with recruitment and training 
plan.

2 OVERVIEW OF SNSA ORGANIZATION AND TASKS 
 
2.1 SNSA ORGANIZATION 
SNSA is an organization, which is divided into several divisions, sections and services. Two of the largest divisions 
are further divided into sections. The 5 main divisions (and their sections) are the following: 
 Nuclear Safety Division 
- Analysis and Licensing Section 
- Operational Safety Section 
 Radiation, Safety and Materials Division 
- Radiation Safety Section 
- Monitoring Section 
- Non-proliferation and Nuclear Security 
 Radiation and Nuclear Safety Inspection Division 
 Emergency Preparedness Division 
 Cyber Security Division 
In addition, SNSA comprises of 2 services, which are: 
 International Cooperation Service, and 
 General Affairs Service. 
Last but not least, there is one director of SNSA, and one position of management system manager reporting 
directly to the SNSA director.

SNSA is a body within the Ministry of Natural Resources and Spatial Planning (MNRSP), and although SNSA is 
functionally autonomous institution it has certain shared services with the Ministry, such as finance and human 
resource services, and is in a way bound to the limitations of resources (financial and human) of the Ministry, 
which are set by the Slovenian Government. According to the MNRSP personnel/staffing plan for 2022 and 2023 
the personnel policy for the SNSA sets a quota of 41 employees. 
The employment of these 41 personnel are financed from the integral state budget. Occasionally SNSA also hires 
one or two interns, but this is for a fixed period, usually up to a year. Likewise, SNSA sometimes employs 
additional one or two staff to work on different SNSA projects (usually international projects of support to other 
newcomer regulators). This staff is also employed on fixed (one or two year) contract. Sometimes these interns 
or staff working on projects are then permanently employed, but only if there are vacancies (if somebody retires 
or leaves SNSA). The number of permanently employed staff always remains the same (41). 
At the moment of implementing this study SNSA employs 42 staff, as there are two interns (one in the Nuclear 
Safety Division and another in the Radiation Safety Division), and one vacant post in the General Affairs Service.

Like most other nuclear regulators, SNSA relies on the help of technical support organizations (TSO). Slovenian 
legislation requires that any major change or more complex administrative/licensing procedure is supported by

independent calculations or analyses to be developed by TSOs when necessary.

 SNSA PROCESSES 
Since 2007, SNSA has in place an integrated management system (IMS), which is based on the process focused-
approach. The processes, which are divided into one management process, eight core processes and one 
supporting process, encompass: 
 Management process 
- Project Management 
- Quality Management System 
- Human Resources and Knowledge Management 
- Financial Resources 
- Measurement & Improvement 
 Control of Nuclear and Radiation Safety, separated into two sub-processes: 
- Control of Nuclear Safety 
- Control of Radiation Safety 
 Inspection 
 Preparation of Legislation 
 Emergency Preparedness 
 Monitoring  
 Annual and National Reports 
 International Cooperation 
 Information Security 
 Infrastructure and Working Environment (support process). 
As pointed in the SNSA IMS Manual [111], the SNSA processes, depending on their size, complexity or 
importance are implemented either as: 
 function-based, or 
 project-based processes. 
Function-based processes are led and implemented within a specific organizational unit (e.g., inspections 
performed by Radiation and Nuclear Safety Inspection Division), while project-based processes are those that 
are implemented within multiple organizational units (e.g., preparation of different reports within the Annual 
and National Reports process). In practice, this division is not strictly applied, since, as will be shown below, all 
processes are carried out within at least two (but mostly several) organizational units.

 ALLOCATION OF TASKS INTO TASK GROUPS 
In the SNSA input document [2], there are 283 different tasks, each associated with one of the following 
processes: 
 Management 
 Control of radiation safety 
 Control of nuclear safety 
 Inspection 
 Preparation of legislation 
 EPR 
 Monitoring 
 Annual and national reports 
 International cooperation 
 IT, infrastructure and work environment 
This list of processes listed in [2] is incomplete, since, as described in 2.2.1, SNSA processes also include 
Information security (or Cyber security), as well as subprocesses Project management, Integrated  management 
system (or IMS), Human resources, Financial resources, and Measurement & Improvement. Thus, many tasks 
related to these missing processes are in [2] connected to Management process, or Infrastructure and work 
environment, or even some other processes. 
In addition, it was noticed that some tasks are connected to certain processes according to who performs them 
and not to which process they logically belong. One such example is a task called “ERDS maintenance”, which is 
completely related to the Emergency Preparedness process (ERDS, or Emergency Response Data System is used 
for monitoring Krško NPP process parameters during emergency events or exercises), but since ERDS is 
maintained by staff from the Nuclear Safety Division it is allocated to the Control of nuclear safety process. 
Due to such inconsistencies it would be hard to get a clear picture of how much time is spent on a certain process 
and who actually works on a certain process. 
Therefore, a very detailed review of each task (in terms of task description) was performed. Each task was 
(re)assigned to the process or subprocess it logically belongs to. At the end the 283 tasks were assigned to the 
following 18 groups of tasks:  
 management 
 radiation safety 
 nuclear safety 
 inspection 
 legislation & regulation 
 EPR 
 monitoring 
 national and other reports 
 international cooperation 
 cyber security & IT 
 IMS 
 human resources 
 communication & reporting 
 main office 
 legal support 
 public procurement 
 research & development (R&D)

2.2.4.3 MONITORING SECTION 
Figure 6 shows the distribution of workload in the Monitoring Section. In contrast to the two previous

Likewise, we can see that larger part of this section resources is dedicated to other task groups, such as “cyber

Figure 6: Distribution of workload in the Monitoring Section by task groups 
The “monitoring” process is the closest to the “function-based” process that would be completely implemented 
within one organizational unit. Monitoring tasks are mostly done within the Monitoring Section with only around 
5% contribution from the Cyber Security Division, which comes in the form of (IT) maintenance support.  
Most of work in the monitoring task group is dedicated to maintenance of the environmental monitoring system 
(68%), which includes exchange of field probes, maintenance and use of release modeling programs, 
maintenance of ROKO database, etc. Smaller part of the work is allotted to development (29%), and even smaller 
for performing field measurements (3%).

Although a smaller number of staff could (and probably does) mean greater efficiency of an organization, this 
does have its own limitations. One of the biggest problems with a smaller RA is overburdening of employees, as 
each expert has to bear a greater workload if compared with larger RAs. Likewise, with a smaller number of 
people it is absolutely necessary to optimize the work, so some tasks cannot be given enough attention or less 
attention is given than it should be. This can also lead to a drop in the quality of the work done, and in the worst 
case to mistakes with more severe consequences. 
Another problem of small number of staff (even with the highest possible efficiency) is ensuring sufficient 
expertise, since in order to ensure adequate nuclear and radiation safety, a variety of competencies are needed, 
which with a smaller number of people is more difficult and sometimes impossible to guarantee (e.g., the 
authors of the study are aware of a fact that SNSA lacks experts in the field of electrical engineering both in 
authorization and supervision field of work). 
In regards to the size of nuclear program it is obvious that the Dutch ANVS would be the most suitable regulatory 
authority for comparison against the SNSA. The second most suitable would be the Bulgarian BNRA, and/or 
Romanian CNCAN, although both of these have reported on having problems with retaining experts, so the 
adequacy of their staff number is questionable. All other regulators (except Belgian) have in recent years 
expanded dramatically the number of their staff mostly to prepare for licensing of construction of the new 
nuclear reactors. 
Out of the three above mentioned RAs, it is apparent that ANVS is the best candidate for the comparison. Besides 
the fact that it regulates the same number of power reactors, the ANVS is most modern, state-of-the-art RA, as 
it was newly established in 2015 based on recommendations of the IAEA IRRS mission from 2014. Not only that, 
ANVS is an example of learning and developing RA, as it has already made several smaller upgrades of its 
organizations with the aim of improving efficiency and obtaining higher level of work quality. In addition, similar 
to SNSA it uses a considerable amount of external expert support in its work, which is a very important aspect 
when comparing the two regulators. In the following chapters ANVS is described, analyzed and compared to the 
SNSA.  
3.1 DESCRIPTION OF ANVS9 
Before 2015 the Ministry of Economic Affairs was the principal responsible authority for conducting the 
regulatory process under the Nuclear Energy Act and for the main functions of the regulatory body, such as 
preparation of legislation, issuing licenses on the radiation protection part of the Nuclear Energy Act, performing 
supervision, etc. Besides the Ministry of Economic Affairs several other entities had responsibilities related to 
Nuclear Energy Act, including Nuclear Installations and Nuclear Safety Directorate, Radiation Protection & 
Society (a special team within the Ministry of Economic Affairs), and The Nuclear Inspectorate. 
In 2015 a new independent organization was established by merging the above-mentioned organizations into 
the so called Authority for Nuclear Safety and Radiation Protection (ANVS). ANVS was first established as a 
directorate within the ministry of Infrastructure and the Water Management, operating under mandate of the 
Minister. In 2017, following approval and entry into effect of the necessary legislation, the ANVS became an 
independent administrative authority, with its own legal authorities.  
ANVS is led by two Board Members who are not civil servants, but have been officially appointed as independent 
Board Members that are in charge of the ANVS. The staff of the ANVS are employed by the Ministry of 
Infrastructure and the Water Management as civil servants but work for the Board. 
The ANVS has the following responsibilities: 
 Granting licences for nuclear facilities in the Netherlands; 
 Regulating all other radiation practices by licensing or notification and registration;

3.3 OPERATION OF THE ANVS AND DIVISION OF RESPONSIBILITIES 
In both main (substantive) departments (Competent Authority Department and Assessment & Advice 
Department) the directors and the team leaders have a decision-making mandate for the areas for which they 
are responsible. 
Team leaders (in consultation with the director) draw up the frameworks of how work is to be performed within 
each team. Within those frameworks, it is the employees who carry out the work on the basis of their 
knowledge, skills and expertise. Team leaders provide leadership based on trust and are responsible for ensuring 
that employees are adequately equipped to perform their duties properly. The “4- or 6-eyes principle” is used 
(work and final results of each employee are reviewed by other employees in the same team; 1 or 2 reviewers, 
depending on the importance of the work performed) to ensure the adequate quality as well as professional 
autonomy of the team members. 
The responsibility for the performance of all statutory tasks is always explicitly assigned to one specific 
department. But this does not mean that all work required for completing that responsibilities are also by

definition carried out within this department. There are many tasks that transcend the boundaries of 
teams/departments, as they require involvement of a whole spectrum of knowledge and expertise. 
In outline, the division of responsibilities is as follows: 
 Employees (as file holders) are the (operational) foundation of the organization and every employee is 
responsible for the quality of his/her work, his/her own further development, making his/her own work 
planning, seeking the necessary cooperation and commitment from colleagues (also from other 
teams/departments) and monitoring the right work processes. When problems in task implementation 
are identified, the employee is responsible for a timely escalation to the team leader. They are also 
responsible for their contribution to the proper functioning of the team. 
 Team leaders make the (tactical) transfer of the management's strategic course into the specific results 
asked of the teams. Team leaders are responsible for the realization of the team annual plans and that 
the realization processes within the teams run smoothly, that employees know what is expected of them 
and what responsibilities they have and that employees are adequately equipped (knowledge and 
competencies). For this purpose, the team leaders receive the responsibility for the human resources of 
all employees in their team. They are the primary contact point for the employees and have insight into 
the employee's range of tasks. They also have a responsibility for the quality of its team products. They 
contribute to the strategic development within the team, which they form together with the department 
director. The team leaders of the two main departments are supposed not to perform case work, but 
are responsible for the effectiveness of their team and personal development/assessment of the 
employees in their teams. 
 Directors translate the long-term ANVS strategy into a strategic course for their own department. They 
do this though the consultation with the board. In this way they give direction to the developments 
within the departments and also coordinate them with their external counterparts, if necessary. They 
are also responsible for the tasks for which the department is responsible (within the context and 
interests of the entire ANVS) and translate this together with the team leaders into specific results for 
the teams. 
 The board is responsible for the long-term strategy of the ANVS as a whole. The board is the figurehead 
of the ANVS to the outside world and represents the ANVS (inter)departmentally, (inter)nationally and 
in the field at administrative level. In this way, the board gives direction to the course of the organization 
at a strategic level. The board has final responsibility for the ANVS and therefore for the realization of 
the mission and the fulfilment of the legal duties of the ANVS. 
 The three directors, together with the board, form the Management Team. The Management Team is 
assisted by a secretary. The formal administrative responsibilities for the functioning of the ANVS as a 
whole lie with the board, however, the actual control takes place within the Management Team. The 
Management Team is making decisions and determining strategy as a collective. It determines and gives 
an integral interpretation to the course of the ANVS, sets out and monitors the main lines and takes care 
of its concrete implementation within the departments. The Management Team also encourages 
cooperation and synergy in the organization. The members of this team are accountable to each other 
and to the organization.

Here it is also important to consider the actual increase in the amount of work during last 20 years, which 
among other includes: 
 Introduction of periodic safety reviews (PSR) - in Slovenia, the first PSR was implemented in 2001 for the 
Krško NPP, which was later followed by PSR of TRIGA research reactor, and other facilities. PSRs, which 
are performed every 10 years, last from 2 to 3 years (plus additional 5 years for improvements' 
implementation oversight), and include up to 20 experts from the regulatory body and thus represent a 
huge additional burden; 
 IRRS and other peer review missions are now performed regularly (as required by the amended EU 
Nuclear Safety Directive). These missions also require the involvement of almost entire regulatory body 
and last several years (i.e., preparation for the mission, implementation of the mission, implementation 
of suggested improvements, implementation of the follow-up mission);  
 EU topical peer reviews were introduced in 2016. These represent another form of peer review, which 
similarly last several years and involve several RA's experts in the preparation of reports, participation 
in peer review of other facilities, several years of reporting on the improvements, plus participation of 
the experts in the development of each peer review topic and review's specifications;  
 New regular reporting have been established, such as reporting to EC, IAEA's Country Nuclear Power 
Profiles (CNPP), regular reporting on events and operating experience in nuclear facilities (e.g., IRS), etc.; 
 There is ever more participation of RA’s experts in various international organizations including IAEA, 
WENRA, ENSREG, HERCA, OECD/NEA and their topical working groups; 
 New nuclear facilities are being built. In the last five years, SNSA has spent a lot of time and effort 
reviewing the applications for issuance of construction licenses for the repository of low- and 
intermediate-level radioactive waste, as well as for the spent fuel dry storage facility; 
 The Radiation Safety Section is responsible for control over the radioactive waste management in 
Slovenia including licensing of new disposal facility that requires detailed knowledge on operational 
safety as well as post-closure safety and for which no additional staff was employed; 
 The use of radioactive sources has increased in last 20 years. If counting radioactive sources’ users in 
Slovenia, one can see ([16] and [17]) that this number has increased by almost 70% (from 175 in 2003 
to 296 in 2022);

Additional justification for the increase of SNSA staff number is the sustainability of knowledge management. 
Highly competent experts are extremely important for maintaining an adequate level of nuclear and radiation 
safety. Within this industry specific knowledge and competencies are used, which are not readily available on 
the labour market (especially in a country with such a small nuclear programme), and which require years of 
schooling and education to acquire. This is precisely why knowledge management is extremely important, and 
why every nuclear regulator must have developed knowledge management processes (which SNSA also has 
within its IMS). Nevertheless, the small number of people in the organization, which must cover all the 
necessary competencies in the fields of nuclear and radiation safety, can represent a big problem in the 
knowledge management process. First, such a large set of competencies as required by a nuclear regulator is 
already difficult (perhaps impossible) to distribute to such a small number of people. In addition, the small 
number of employees further complicates the proper transfer of knowledge, since competencies for which 
backup is required cannot be transferred due to the workload and staff shortage. Thus, with each departure of 
an employee (e.g., retirement, leaving for another job, etc.), knowledge is lost and it is almost impossible to 
replace it in a relatively short time (three to five years).

6.2 SNSA TRAINING PLAN 
In addition to basic education, additional education and training is equally, if not more important, with which 
personnel acquire the specific competencies necessary for work in the RA, such as the above-mentioned 
nuclear technology, reactor physics, radiation protection, physical protection, deterministic and probabilistic

safety analyses, knowledge of severe accidents, fire protection, environmental qualification, and in addition to 
these, knowledge of legislation and regulation in the field, systems of nuclear facilities, event analysis, aging 
management, surveillance activities, radioactive releases and assessment of effective doses, transport of 
radioactive material, use of radioactive sources, radioactive waste, emergency preparedness, etc. 
Some of this knowledge is usually acquired by attending various IAEA workshops and training courses, but it is 
very important and beneficial if the RA developpes its own training system, as this speeds up the acquisition of 
relevant competencies required for the independent work of experts. 
SNSA has developed its “Systematic Approach to Training” (SAT), which is based on IAEA TECDOC 1254 
“Training the staff of the regulatory body for nuclear facilities: A competency framework”, and adjusted to later 
issued IAEA Safety Reports Series No. 79, “Managing Regulatory Body Competence”, and TECDOC 1757, 
“Methodology for the Systematic Assessment of the Regulatory Competence Needs (SARCoN) for Regulatory 
Bodies of Nuclear Installations”. SNSA's SAT (or SAT-URSJV) is established and described in the SNSA's internal 
procedure OP 1.60, “Skrb za razvoj sodelavcev URSJV” [19]. 
SAT-URSJV is used for: 
 identification, analysis and optimization of tasks that need to be performed by SNSA; 
 identification of competencies that are required for each task; 
 distribution of tasks by processes, organizational units and workplaces; 
 regular annual monitoring of staff competence; 
 management of trainings for acquisition and maintenance of competencies; 
 identification of the need for new personnel. 
The OP 1.60 (Attachment 3) also includes different scope of training plans depending on the workplace (e.g., 
in the Main Office, Nuclear Safety Division, Inspection Division, etc., and which tasks it includes), and whether 
it is a new employee or an experienced one (i.e., initial training, basic / advanced training, regular trainings, 
etc.). The Contractor received the draft of the 6th revision of the OP 1.60 procedure as an input document, 
which shows that Attachment 3 “Training plan” has only been newly added to this procedure. The Contractor 
sees this extension of the procedure with a detailed training plan as a good decision, as it is in a way the basis 
of systematic employee training. 
Although SNSA's SAT is a very well established and will be further substantially improved with the inclusion of 
the new Attachment on the Training Plan, the Contractor still suggests two important possible improvements: 
1. Procedure OP 1.60 places too little emphasis on training of new employees, as there are only two short 
paragraphs about their involvement and training. In contrast to this, the draft procedure ON 1.60.x 
“Training Plan for Inspectors” [20] gives much more attention to new employees' training with a whole 
chapter devoted to it (Chapter 3.4). It is suggested that the procedure OP 1.60 be supplemented in a 
similar way. Special emphasis should be given to the: 
- (mandatory) appointment of mentors to new employees; 
- development of individual training plan, which should include time plan of the training, training 
modules, names of responsible trainers, etc. (a draft of such training plan could be added to the 
OP 1.60 as an attachment); 
- reporting on implementation of the plan to SAT-URSJV manager and the superiors, etc. 
2. Currently SAT-URSJV is managed by the head of the Inspection Division, who naturally devotes 
relatively little time to this (as can be seen from [2], the head of Inspection Division devotes 3% of his 
time (7.5 days a year) to the management of SAT-URSJV). Since the regular training of employees is one 
of the key pillars of nuclear and radiation safety, it is suggested that special attention be paid to this, 
namely by appointing a person who will devote at least 30-50% of his/her time to SAT-URSJV. Of course, 
this cannot be the head of Inspection Division, instead it is suggested to appoint (and train) a person 
from some other organizational unit (e.g., Nuclear Safety Support unit, Radiation Safety, EPR division, 
Main office, etc.). This would be sufficient for a stable organization. However, for the reorganization 
options, a full time position for HR and training would be needed to cope with the high volume of work 
to implement the reorganization.

7 CONCLUSION 
If one takes a close look at SNSA, one sees a well-established, experienced organization that with a small staff 
is being responsible and implements tasks for which other EU regulatory bodies may have available 100, 200 
or even more employees. One of the key factors here is probably the experience of the team, as the turnover 
rate of the organization is very small, which shows in the average experience of staff with the nuclear regulator 
to be around 15 years. 
In addition to that the SNSA already in 2007 developed its integrated management system (IMS), which today 
after 16 years of use counts around 160 different procedures. The IMS directs the workflow of the SNSA 
processes in a manner to use the resources (human and financial) as efficiently as possible. Likewise, the 
established IMS procedures help the newcomers in their learning curve and to adapt to SNSA workflow as 
quickly and effectively as possible. 
Nevertheless, it is necessary to ask why other European regulatory authorities need so many more people than 
SNSA, since all comparable EU regulators are several times larger, and not only regulators in Eastern countries 
(to which we usually attribute inefficiency), but also in Western ones, such as the France, Finland, Sweden or 
the Netherlands.  
The number of tasks performed by EU regulatory authorities has increased significantly over the past 20 years, 
including: 
 introduction of periodic safety reviews,  
 IRRS and other peer review missions are now performed regularly (as required by amended EU Nuclear 
Safety Directive),  
 EU topical peer reviews were introduced in 2016,  
 new regular reporting have been established (such as reporting to EC, CNPP, IRS),  
 there is more participation in various international organizations and working groups,  
 new nuclear facilities are being built,  
 new areas of work have been introduced (e.g., cyber security, control of consumer products and building 
materials, safety culture, critical infrastructure risk assessments, etc.),  
 number of radioactive sources and their users have increased dramatically (in Slovenia by 70%),  
 the licensing procedures have changed, requiring detailed review and assessment as well as record 
keeping of health certificates, education and RP training certificates for all exposed workers, 
 the intervals for reporting to EC in the field of civil protection have been shortened (now reporting every 
3 years instead of 5), 
 independent verification monitoring of operating facilities was introduced (at the request of the EC),  
 regulation is constantly being benchmarked, harmonized and upgraded, while previously, once 
established, the regulation lasted tens of years,  
 in addition to that, SNSA took over the complete maintenance of the early warning network that was 
formerly done by the meteorological service. 
 
In this period most of the EU regulators compared in this study have increased their number of staff (e.g., 
Slovakia, Hungary, and Finland by 48%, 129%, and 337% respectively). Meanwhile, the number of employees 
at SNSA remained the same or even decreased. 
The argument of greater efficiency may be valid to some extent, but beyond that the quality of work most 
probably suffers. 
 
This study provides a detailed overview of the SNSA organization, the tasks performed by the SNSA, and the 
distribution of the work of individual organizational units and the tasks they participate in performing.

An overview is given of all EU nuclear regulatory authorities, whose tasks also include the supervision of nuclear 
power plants. As the most comparable organization the Dutch regulatory body, ANVS was chosen for several 
reasons: 
 ANVS supervises the operation of only one nuclear power plant (as does SNSA); 
 In general, the nuclear program in the Netherlands is comparable to Slovenia (with minor differences, 
which are properly taken into account in the analysis); 
 The Netherlands is considered an efficient country with an efficient state apparatus; 
 ANVS is a newly established, modern administrative body that was created in 2015 taking into account 
all the guidelines of previous IRRS missions. 
By comparing the two regulatory authorities and taking due account of the differences, such as the difference 
in the size of the nuclear programs of the two countries, the different workload in relation to the number of 
users of radiation sources, SRPA's supervision over radiation safety in healthcare and veterinary medicine, 
sharing of certain support tasks with different ministries in Slovenia, etc., a number of SNSA employees is 
determined that would be comparable to the number of employees at ANVS with approximately the same 
amount of work. This new number of SNSA employees is 68 (not counting director). 
On the one hand, this number seems large, especially if considering that it means a 66% increase in the number 
of SNSA staff. On the other hand, it is a much smaller increase compared to other EU regulatory bodies, since 
in many of them the number has doubled in the last 20 years, while some of them have increased their staff 
by a factor of three or more. If in addition the actual increase in the amount of work during these years is taken 
into account, which other regulators followed by gradual increase of staff number, while SNSA kept the number 
of employees unchanged, it is obvious that the proposed increase in the number of staff is logical and justified. 
The proposal for additional employment naturally gives greater priority to increasing the competence of the 
regulatory authority, therefore most new staff should be distributed to expert organizational units dealing with 
nuclear and radiation safety and inspection of nuclear and radiation facilities. 
Since it is practically impossible to increase the number of employees to such a number at once, firstly due to 
the limitations that the labour market presents, and on the other hand, because new hires represent an 
additional burden on existing employees who have to train the new staff, a proposal is given to gradually 
increase the number of employees, in three to four years. 
 
The study shows another aspect that should be taken into account in order to ensure the highest possible 
efficiency and thus the validity of the new number of SNSA employees, and that is the large fragmentation of 
the SNSA. Currently, SNSA has its 41 employees distributed in 12 individual organizational units with 12 
individual managers (not counting director), who manage the other 29 people (i.e., 29% of employees are 
managers). Even more striking is that several of these managers lead only two people, and even worse, one or 
none. In contrast, as the study shows, at ANVS only about 10% is the management staff, as 16 managers lead 
the other 139 employees. Therefore, besides increasing the number of staff as presented in this study, another 
suggestion is to do a reorganization of SNSA's organizational units with the main goal to reduce number of 
organizational units and thus optimize SNSA management. 
A special pillar for ensuring the effectiveness and competence of a nuclear regulatory authority is its training 
program, where SNSA certainly does not lag behind other comparable regulators. The SNSA’s training program, 
which is established within the framework of IMS, includes initial trainings that are separated by organizational 
units and job positions, as well as regular, refresher trainings. Generally, the SNSA's training program is very 
well developed based on the state-of-the-art IAEA standards, which enables a relatively quick establishment of 
the initial qualification of the staff as well as ensures long-term development of competencies. 
A thorough review of the SNSA's training plan was done within this study giving two proposals for its 
enhancement, the implementation of which should further contribute to even more systematic training of new 
people and thus to faster provision of the appropriate competencies needed for independent work of the new 
staff.

competencies of the SNSA as the nuclear regulatory authority, greater efficiency, and ultimately higher level 
of nuclear and radiation safety in Slovenia.
